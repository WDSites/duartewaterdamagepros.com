Mold Remediation Cost in Duarte, CA

No two mold jobs price the same. Here are the factors that move a bid, what a written scope should list, and how to compare quotes fairly.

By the Duarte Water Damage Pros team · Published October 27, 2026 · Updated October 27, 2026

☎ (626) 879-0276

Mold remediation cost in Duarte, CA depends on the job, and any company that quotes a number over the phone without seeing the damage is guessing. This guide does not give prices. It explains the factors that move a bid, what a clear written scope looks like, and how to compare two bids so you are comparing the same work.

A low number and a high number can both be honest if they cover different things. The trick is to find out what is inside each one.

The factors that set the price

Mold work is priced on labor, equipment and rebuild, and each factor below changes one of those three.

Cost factors, why each matters and what to ask
Cost factorWhy it mattersWhat to ask
Area sizeMore square footage means more labor, plastic, cleaning and disposal.How many square feet or linear feet are included in the scope?
ContainmentPlastic barriers, zipper doors and HEPA negative air machines take time and equipment to set up.Is containment included, and how many air machines will run?
Materials removedCutting out drywall, insulation, flooring or cabinets adds demolition, bagging and disposal.Which materials are coming out, and who disposes of them?
RebuildReplacing drywall, paint, baseboard and flooring is separate from remediation.Is rebuild included or a separate contract?
TestingSampling before or after work is optional and often done by a separate company.Is testing included, and who does it?
AccessAttics, crawlspaces and tight walls take longer and need more protection.Are there access problems that could change the scope?
Drying timeEquipment days add up when moisture is slow to leave.How many days of drying are planned, and what is the dry target?

Notice that the biggest swing is usually not the mold itself. It is how much material has to be removed and rebuilt. A small patch on tile might need a wipe and a dry. A wall that has been wet for weeks may need demolition, containment, drying and a rebuild.

What a written scope should list

Get the scope on paper. A real scope removes the surprises that cause disputes later. It should name each item below.

  • The rooms and surfaces involved, with approximate sizes.
  • The standard being followed. ANSI/IICRC S520 is the published mold remediation standard, and you can ask any company if it follows it.
  • How containment will be set up and whether negative air will run.
  • Which materials will be removed and which will be cleaned.
  • How the area will be cleaned, such as HEPA vacuuming and damp wiping.
  • How the work will be checked at the end, such as moisture readings and a visual walkthrough.
  • What is not included, such as rebuild, painting, or testing.
  • The CSLB license number, payment schedule and start date.

If a bid is one line with a single total, ask for an itemized version. It costs nothing to ask and it tells you a lot about the company.

How to compare two bids

Put the two scopes side by side and go line by line. A higher bid is not always worse. A lower bid is not always a bargain.

  1. Match the scope. Are both bids covering the same rooms, materials and sizes?
  2. Check containment. One bid may include negative air and the other may not.
  3. Look at the rebuild. One may stop at demolition while the other includes drywall and paint.
  4. Compare the end check. Does each describe how the work is verified?
  5. Read the exclusions. The fine print is where extras live.
  6. Ask about change orders. What happens if more damage is found behind the wall?

What a very low bid might leave out

A very low bid is a prompt for questions, not an automatic red flag. But here are the usual gaps.

  • No containment or no negative air machine, so dust spreads while the work is done.
  • Surface cleaning only, with porous drywall or insulation left in place.
  • No drying and no moisture readings, so the wall stays damp and growth can return.
  • No disposal line, or disposal left to you.
  • No rebuild, and no mention that it is excluded.
  • No license number on the contract.

The cheapest-looking bid can turn into the most expensive one once the missing steps are added as change orders. Ask what happens if the moisture is still there after the work.

Cost also connects back to drying. If a leak is caught early, water mitigation can prevent the need for remediation at all, which is the lowest-cost outcome. Once growth is present, our mold remediation page describes the scope in more detail.

Why the order of work matters for cost

Cost climbs when steps are done out of order. If drywall is rebuilt before the cavity is dry, the new wall gets wet and the job is repeated. If containment is skipped, dust lands in clean rooms and cleaning spreads. Ask each company for the sequence of work, from source repair to drying to removal to rebuild. A clear sequence usually means fewer change orders, and fewer surprises on the final invoice.

Insurance, in general terms

The California Department of Insurance says homeowners policies do not cover flood damage but do cover other kinds of water damage, and that mold caused by a covered peril may be covered. If you suspect mold after a leak, tell your adjuster immediately and check your policy. Keep receipts for reasonable temporary repairs. Coverage depends on the cause and the policy language, so ask your insurer before you sign a contract.

Duarte local note: owners, renters and the contract

Census QuickFacts puts Duarte at 61.0 percent owner-occupied, which means roughly 39 percent of households rent. The median owner-occupied home value is $779,400. If you own, a written scope protects a large asset, so get it before demolition starts. If you rent, notify your landlord in writing and keep a copy before you hire anyone.

On the contract itself, California rules are specific. Work of $1,000 or more in labor and materials needs a licensed contractor, the contract must show the CSLB license number, and LA County DCBA guidance says not to pay more than $1,000 or 10 percent as a down payment, whichever is less. Mold cleanup must also follow EPA and Cal/OSHA rules.
